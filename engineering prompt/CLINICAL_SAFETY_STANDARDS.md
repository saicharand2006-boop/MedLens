# MedLens — AI-Powered Clinical Information Intelligence
## Mandatory Product Behavior & Clinical Safety Standards

> **GOVERNING CONSTITUTION**: These guidelines are mandatory product rules. When functionality, UI design, AI prompts, data processing, summaries, or user flows conflict with these guidelines, these guidelines take priority.

---

## 1. PRODUCT PURPOSE

MedLens exists to:

**Collect, organize, extract, structure, explain, compare, and review medical information.**

MedLens does NOT exist to:

* Diagnose diseases
* Replace physicians
* Prescribe treatments
* Recommend medication changes
* Determine appropriate medication dosage
* Provide definitive clinical conclusions
* Act as an emergency medical service
* Make decisions on behalf of healthcare professionals

The system must always remain an information intelligence and organization tool.

---

## 2. SOURCE-OF-TRUTH PRINCIPLE

When presenting medical information, preserve the distinction between:

### USER-PROVIDED
Information entered manually by the user.

### SOURCE-EXTRACTED
Information extracted from a medical report or other uploaded source.

### AI-GENERATED
Information produced by AI through summarization, organization, comparison, or explanation.

**These categories must never be silently merged.**

Whenever feasible, users should be able to identify where information came from.

---

## 3. PROVENANCE IS A FIRST-CLASS DATA ATTRIBUTE

Important medical facts should maintain provenance.

A medical data item should conceptually support attributes such as:

* Value
* Unit
* Date
* Source
* Source type
* Extraction status
* Confidence
* Verification status
* Original value
* Edited value
* User correction history where appropriate

A user must be able to understand whether information:
* came directly from them,
* was extracted from a report,
* was modified by them,
* or was generated by AI.

**Never present AI-generated information as if it were directly documented in the source.**

---

## 4. REFERENCE-RANGE RULE

This is a critical requirement.

When evaluating laboratory values, MedLens must use **only the reference range provided by the source report**.

**Never invent, assume, substitute, estimate, or retrieve a reference range merely because a test is commonly associated with one.**

If the source provides:
* **Test:** Hemoglobin
* **Value:** 12.4
* **Unit:** g/dL
* **Reference range:** 13.0–17.0

then the system may identify the value as below the reference range stated in that report.

If the source does NOT provide a reference range, display:

> **Reference range not provided in source report**

**Do not infer whether the value is low, normal, or high.**

---

## 5. REFERENCE RANGE DISPLAY

Whenever a reference range is available, preserve it exactly enough to remain faithful to the source.

Preserve:
* Numerical limits
* Units
* Qualifiers
* Age/sex-specific ranges
* Other contextual information

Do not silently convert or normalize a range if doing so could change its meaning.

If multiple reference ranges exist within a report, preserve the relationship between:
**Test → Result → Applicable reference range**

---

## 6. LAB VALUE STATUS

The preferred status vocabulary is:

* **Within reported range**
* **Below reported range**
* **Above reported range**
* **Reference range unavailable**

Do not automatically replace these with stronger clinical interpretations such as:
* Disease
* Deficiency
* Infection
* Dangerous
* Critical condition

unless such wording is explicitly present in the source and is clearly attributed to the source.

Even when a value lies outside the report's range, MedLens should describe the relationship to the range rather than diagnose the underlying cause.

---

## 7. DO NOT PROVIDE DIAGNOSIS

Never produce definitive diagnostic statements.

* **Unsafe:** “This means you have anemia.”
* **Preferred:** “The report shows a hemoglobin value below the reference range provided by the report.”

The system may organize and summarize documented observations but must not turn laboratory observations into unsupported diagnoses.

---

## 8. DO NOT PROVIDE TREATMENT RECOMMENDATIONS

Never recommend:
* Medication changes
* Dosage changes
* Starting medication
* Stopping medication
* Increasing medication
* Decreasing medication
* Treatment plans
* Specific therapeutic interventions

Do not tell the user what medication they should take.

When users ask treatment-oriented questions, the product should remain within its role as an information organizer and encourage professional review where appropriate.

---

## 9. DO NOT OVERRIDE CLINICIANS OR SOURCE DOCUMENTS

The system must not imply that AI is more authoritative than:
* A clinician
* A laboratory report
* A prescribing professional
* The original medical source

When information conflicts, clearly surface the inconsistency.

**Never silently choose a value simply because the AI believes it is more plausible.**

---

## 10. CONFLICT DETECTION

When records contain inconsistent information, surface the conflict.

Examples:
* A medication appears as active in one record and discontinued in another.
* A laboratory test has different values on the same date.
* Two reports contain different demographic information.

Do not silently reconcile conflicting information unless the reconciliation is explicitly supported by source information or user confirmation.

Use language such as:

> **“Conflicting information detected. Review the source records.”**

---

## 11. MISSING INFORMATION

Missing information must remain missing.

Never fabricate:
* Test values
* Reference ranges
* Dates
* Medication doses
* Symptoms
* Diagnoses
* Patient history
* Physician observations

A missing field should be represented as:
* **Not provided**
* **Unknown**
* **Not found in source**

rather than filled with an educated guess.

---

## 12. UNCERTAINTY

AI extraction can be imperfect.

Represent uncertainty explicitly. Useful states include:
* **High confidence**
* **Medium confidence**
* **Low confidence**
* **Needs review**

Confidence should reflect extraction/retrieval certainty, not medical severity.

**Do not say:** “90% chance the patient has X.” when the underlying system merely has confidence in text extraction. These are fundamentally different concepts.

---

## 13. AI SUMMARY RULES

The patient-friendly summary should answer:

> **“What information is present in the available records?”**

It may summarize:
* Patient-reported symptoms
* Documented conditions
* Reported medications
* Laboratory findings
* Report observations
* Changes across reports
* Missing or conflicting information

It should avoid turning those observations into diagnoses.

The summary should clearly indicate when content is:
* **Reported**
* **Extracted**
* **AI summarized**

---

## 14. SUMMARY LANGUAGE

Prefer precise language such as:
* “The report records…”
* “The uploaded document lists…”
* “The laboratory result is…”
* “The value is above the reference range provided in the report.”
* “The available records show…”

Avoid:
* “This proves…”
* “This means you definitely have…”
* “You should…”
* “You need to take…”
* “This is dangerous…”

unless such wording is explicitly documented by the source and is being faithfully attributed.

---

## 15. USER QUESTIONS

When answering questions about their records, prioritize:
1. What the source says
2. What was extracted
3. What can be directly compared
4. What is missing
5. What remains uncertain

**Do not fill uncertainty with speculation.**

---

## 16. HUMAN VERIFICATION

Users must be able to review and correct extracted information.

For important fields, provide a mechanism to:
* Verify
* Edit
* Reject
* Flag
* Compare against source

When a user changes an extracted value, preserve enough provenance to distinguish:
**Original extracted value** from **User-corrected value**

Do not silently make it appear as though the corrected value came from the source document.

---

## 17. SOURCE DOCUMENT ACCESS

Whenever possible, make the original source easy to inspect.

A useful interaction is:
**Structured result → View source → Locate source evidence → Verify → Accept / Edit**

This is especially important for:
* Laboratory values
* Medication information
* Dates
* Diagnoses appearing in source records
* Physician observations

---

## 18. DOCUMENT EXTRACTION

When extracting from reports, preserve the source structure as much as practical.

Extract relevant information such as:
* Test name
* Value
* Unit
* Reference range
* Date
* Observation
* Report section
* Source document

Do not convert ambiguous text into definitive structured data. For example, if the document contains uncertain OCR, flag the field for review rather than confidently inventing a value.

---

## 19. OCR / EXTRACTION ERRORS

Medical documents can contain:
* Poor scans
* Handwriting
* Formatting issues
* Tables
* Multiple columns
* Misaligned values
* OCR mistakes

The system must treat extracted text as potentially fallible. High-risk or ambiguous fields should enter a review state.

**Never hide extraction uncertainty behind polished UI.**

---

## 20. COMPARISON RULES

When comparing reports:

Compare documented information directly.

For example:
* **January 10:** Hemoglobin: 12.4 g/dL | Reference range: 13.0–17.0 g/dL
* **March 15:** Hemoglobin: 13.1 g/dL | Reference range: 13.0–17.0 g/dL

The product may state that the recorded value increased from 12.4 to 13.1.

It should not conclude:
“The patient recovered from anemia.”
unless that diagnosis and conclusion are explicitly documented by an appropriate source.

---

## 21. MEDICATION INFORMATION

Medications should be represented as documented facts.

Possible attributes include:
* Name
* Strength
* Frequency
* Route
* Date
* Source
* Status if explicitly documented

Do not infer:
* Why the drug was prescribed
* Whether the dose is appropriate
* Whether the patient should continue it
* Whether two medications should be combined
* Whether the patient should stop it

---

## 22. PATIENT-FRIENDLY LANGUAGE

Medical information should be understandable without oversimplifying it into misinformation.

Use:
* “Reference range”
* “Value”
* “Reported by the laboratory”
* “Source document”
* “Needs review”

instead of unnecessary clinical jargon.

Where medical terminology is necessary, provide a plain-language explanation without introducing unsupported conclusions.

---

## 23. SAFETY BOUNDARY

MedLens should clearly communicate its role.

A suitable product-level statement is:

> **“MedLens helps organize and explain information found in your records. It does not provide medical diagnosis or treatment advice.”**

This should not become annoying or repetitive. Safety messaging should appear at meaningful points without overwhelming the interface.

---

## 24. PRIVACY

Treat all patient information as sensitive.

The product should minimize unnecessary exposure of:
* Names
* Contact information
* Medical conditions
* Laboratory results
* Medication details
* Uploaded medical documents

Do not use sensitive information for unrelated application features.

Avoid exposing patient information in:
* Logs
* Debug output
* URLs
* Analytics payloads
* Error messages
* Screenshots or previews that are not necessary

---

## 25. AUTHENTICATION & ACCESS CONTROL

Where authentication exists:
* Users should only access records they are authorized to view.
* Patient data should not be globally accessible simply because an identifier is known.
* Design the data model around explicit ownership and access control.

---

## 26. AUDITABILITY

Important modifications should be explainable.

Where practical, maintain a conceptual history of:
* Who entered information
* What was extracted
* What was edited
* When it was changed
* Whether the source was verified

The goal is not surveillance. The goal is traceability and trust.

---

## 27. ACCESSIBILITY

Medical information must remain accessible to users with different abilities.

Never rely solely on:
* Color
* Icons
* Position
* Animation

to communicate medical status. Always provide textual meaning.

For example:
Do not show only a red dot. Show: **Above reported range** and optionally provide a visual indicator.

---

## 28. VISUAL SAFETY

Avoid alarming visual language.

Do not make every out-of-range value look like an emergency. An out-of-range laboratory result is not automatically an emergency.

Visual hierarchy should communicate:
**Information → context → source → status**
rather than:
**RED = DANGER**

---

## 29. EMERGENCY / HIGH-RISK QUESTIONS

MedLens is not an emergency medical service.

If a user describes an urgent health situation, the system should not attempt to diagnose it through the product.

The UI should direct the user toward appropriate professional or emergency medical assistance rather than producing a false sense of certainty.

---

## 30. PRODUCT TRUST PRINCIPLE

**Never optimize for perceived intelligence at the expense of truth.**

* It is better for MedLens to say: **“I could not confidently extract this value.”** than to produce a polished but incorrect result.
* It is better to say: **“Reference range not provided.”** than to invent one.
* It is better to say: **“The records contain conflicting information.”** than to silently choose one.
* It is better to say: **“The report documents this finding.”** than to turn it into an unsupported diagnosis.

---

## 31. AI PROMPTING PRINCIPLE

All internal AI prompts should encourage:
* Grounded extraction
* Structured output
* Explicit uncertainty
* Source attribution
* No hallucination
* No invented medical data
* No invented reference ranges
* No diagnosis
* No treatment recommendations
* No medication changes
* Human review for ambiguity

AI should be instructed to return missing information explicitly instead of guessing.

---

## 32. STRUCTURED DATA OVER FREEFORM TEXT

The system should prefer structured data whenever possible.

Instead of storing:
“Blood results seem generally okay except the hemoglobin appears somewhat low.”

store structured fields:
* **Test:** Hemoglobin
* **Value:** 12.4
* **Unit:** g/dL
* **Reference Range:** 13.0–17.0
* **Status:** Below reported range
* **Date:** 2026-08-20
* **Source:** CBC_Report_August.pdf
* **Provenance:** Extracted
* **Verification:** Needs review

Then generate human-readable explanations from the structured record.

---

## 33. PRODUCT HIERARCHY

When there is a conflict between competing product objectives, prioritize in this order:

1. **Patient/user safety**
2. **Data accuracy**
3. **Provenance and traceability**
4. **Privacy/security**
5. **Human review**
6. **Understandability**
7. **Accessibility**
8. **Reliability**
9. **Performance**
10. **Visual polish**
11. **Novelty**

**Never sacrifice the first six for visual impressiveness.**

---

## 34. DEFINITION OF A SUCCESSFUL MEDLENS EXPERIENCE

A successful user should be able to answer:
* What information is in this record?
* Where did each important piece come from?
* Which information did I provide?
* Which information was extracted?
* Which information was generated by AI?
* Which values are within or outside the report's own reference ranges?
* Which fields still need review?
* What changed between reports?
* What information is missing or conflicting?
* Can I verify an important result against the original source?

If the interface can answer these questions clearly, it is serving the core MedLens mission.

---

## 35. FINAL RESPONSIBLE AI PRINCIPLE

**MedLens should never try to sound smarter than the evidence.**

The product's competitive advantage is not:
> *“The AI knows your health.”*

It is:
> *“MedLens turns fragmented medical information into a structured, understandable, traceable record while making uncertainty and source information visible.”*

That principle must guide the product, UX, AI prompts, database design, extraction pipeline, visual language, and every major feature.
